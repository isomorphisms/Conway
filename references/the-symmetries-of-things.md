## Chapter-by-chapter summary

### Part I — Symmetries of finite objects and plane repeating patterns

**1. Symmetries**  
An introduction by pictures before formalism. The chapter teaches the reader to recognize mirrors, rotations, rosettes, friezes, planar repetitions, and spherical repetitions as manifestations of the same underlying question: what motions preserve an object or pattern?

**2. Planar Patterns**  
The book develops Conway's visual vocabulary for planar symmetry. Mirror lines, rotation centers, glide behavior, and translations are treated as a small collection of geometric features from which the symmetry of a wallpaper pattern can be read.

**3. The Magic Theorem**  
The central classification idea appears. Each kind of symmetry feature is assigned a numerical “cost,” and a plane pattern has total cost 2. Conway's orbifold signature packages the relevant features into a compact symbol, giving an unusually concise route to the 17 wallpaper symmetry types.

**4. The Spherical Patterns**  
The same method is applied to symmetry on the sphere. The chapter classifies spherical symmetry types, connects the geometric catalogue with finite groups, and explains the restrictions that distinguish possible spherical signatures from impossible ones.

**5. Frieze Patterns**  
One-dimensional repetition inside the plane is isolated as its own problem. The seven frieze types emerge from the same feature-counting viewpoint, making the usual catalogue feel like a small theorem rather than seven unrelated cases.

**6. Why the Magic Theorems Work**  
The intuitive classification is given a topological explanation. Symmetric patterns are folded to quotient surfaces, Euler characteristic enters, and the numerical “cost” rule is derived rather than merely observed for friezes and plane patterns.

**7. Euler's Map Theorem**  
A focused treatment of Euler's formula for maps on surfaces. The chapter develops Euler characteristic carefully enough to support the orbifold calculations that follow and shows how familiar surfaces acquire their characteristic values.

**8. Classification of Surfaces**  
The book builds surfaces from handles and crosscaps and explains why these constructions suffice. This is the topology needed to know what quotient surfaces can occur when symmetry folds a pattern onto a smaller underlying space.

**9. Orbifolds**  
The informal notation is placed in its proper geometric setting. An orbifold is the quotient surface together with the singular data left by rotations, mirrors, and related symmetry. This chapter makes explicit that Conway's compact signatures are not merely mnemonic labels but descriptions of genuine quotient geometry.

### Part II — Color symmetry, group theory, and tilings

**10. Presenting Presentations**  
The geometric features of an orbifold are translated into generators and relations. Reflections, rotations, and other motions become algebraic generators, so the symmetry group can be recovered from the same visual data that produced its signature.

**11. Twofold Colorations**  
The authors study symmetries that may interchange two colors rather than preserve each color separately. The chapter classifies twofold color symmetry in the plane and connects the resulting structures with subgroups and dualities.

**12. Threefold Colorings of Plane Patterns**  
The two-color story is extended to three colors. The essential new ingredient is that symmetries act by permutations of three colors, so stabilizers, kernels, and the permutation group of the colors become part of the classification.

**13. Other Primefold Colorings**  
The color-symmetry machinery is generalized from three colors to prime numbers of colors. Special arithmetic behavior associated with square and triangular lattices appears in the Gaussian and Eisensteinian cases, tying wallpaper geometry to elementary algebraic-number-theoretic structure.

**14. Searching for Relations**  
A deeper justification of group presentations. The chapter explains how relations among geometric generators are found, why the listed relations are sufficient, how redundant descriptions can be simplified, and how apparently different presentations can encode the same group.

**15. Types of Tilings**  
The emphasis shifts from symmetry groups to the combinatorics of tiles. Heesch types describe how a tile is surrounded; isohedral types describe tilings whose symmetry group acts transitively on tiles. The chapter organizes tilings by these stronger structural distinctions rather than just by wallpaper group.

**16. Abstract Groups**  
A compact excursion into finite-group structure: cyclic and abelian groups, direct products, extensions, dihedral and quaternionic families, extraspecial groups, and the number of groups of a given order. The point is to connect the very concrete symmetry groups seen earlier with the wider abstract landscape of group theory.

### Part III — Repeating patterns in other spaces

**17. Introducing Hyperbolic Groups**  
The classification program moves into hyperbolic geometry. The reader learns how hyperbolic patterns are represented in the plane, how negative Euler characteristic changes the bookkeeping, and how orbifold signatures continue to classify symmetry when Euclidean restrictions disappear.

**18. More on Hyperbolic Groups**  
The hyperbolic theory is pushed further: when different signatures represent equivalent structures, which signatures actually exist, how they can be constructed and enumerated, and how the picture fits into Thurston's geometrization viewpoint.

**19. Archimedean Tilings**  
The book studies vertex-transitive tessellations and develops a permutation-symbol method for encoding them. Classical Euclidean Archimedean tilings are placed beside their much larger hyperbolic family, making clear which features are exceptional to Euclidean space.

**20. Generalized Schläfli Symbols**  
The familiar `{p,q}` notation for regular tessellations is generalized using flags and flag adjacency. The resulting language can describe structures far beyond regular polygons and polyhedra while retaining a systematic combinatorial core.

**21. Naming Archimedean and Catalan Polyhedra and Tilings**  
Operations such as truncation, stellation-like “kis” operations, snubbing, and duality are organized into a naming system. Rather than memorizing a zoo of polyhedra and tessellations, the reader learns to see them as relatives generated by transformations of simpler parents.

**22. The 35 “Prime” Space Groups**  
Three-dimensional crystallographic symmetry begins. The authors classify a foundational set of space groups using translation lattices and point groups, separate the plenary and quarter groups, and explain why the resulting list is complete.

**23. Objects with Prime Symmetry**  
The abstract space groups are made concrete through structures: lattice Voronoi cells, salt and diamond, bubble packings, infinite polyhedra, atomic nets, and stick constructions. This is one of the book's strongest demonstrations that symmetry classification is not detached formalism but a language for real spatial organization.

**24. Flat Universes**  
Euclidean space is quotiented by fixed-point-free symmetry groups to produce flat manifolds, or platycosms. The torus and Klein bottle appear among a systematic collection of compact flat universes, connecting crystallographic symmetry to global topology.

**25. The 184 Composite Space Groups**  
The remaining three-dimensional space groups are assembled and classified. A major practical issue is aliasing: different-looking constructions or names can describe the same group. The chapter completes the passage from the prime cases to the full crystallographic catalogue considered by the authors.

**26. Higher Still**  
The final chapter opens the door to four and higher dimensions: four-dimensional point groups, regular and Archimedean polytopes, star-polytopes, reflection groups, hemicubes, the Gosset series, and the continuation of symmetry theory beyond ordinary space. It functions less as an endpoint than as an invitation to keep climbing.

## Recommendation

### A letter in favor of owning this book

To anyone deciding whether *The Symmetries of Things* is worth the money:

Yes—if the price is within your means, this book should be on your shelf.

There are many good mathematics books that transmit results, and many beautiful mathematics books that transmit pictures. This one does something rarer: it gives you a new way to **see**. After reading its treatment of mirrors, gyration points, orbifolds, tilings, lattices, and space groups, a wallpaper, a crystal, a tessellation, a polyhedron, or a repeating decorative pattern is no longer just an example of symmetry. You have a compact language for asking what kind of symmetry it has, why that type is possible, how it relates to other types, and what algebraic or topological object is hiding underneath it.

The book is also unusually worth owning physically. Its illustrations are not decoration added after the mathematics; they are part of the mathematical argument. This is a volume to open at random, leave beside a desk, show another person, return to after learning more topology or group theory, and keep finding new things in. The later chapters make it broad enough to function almost as an atlas of the subject, while the opening chapters contain one of the clearest and most memorable entrances into orbifolds and planar symmetry ever written.

It is expensive. That is the only serious reason I would hesitate to tell someone to buy it. If purchasing it would strain your budget, use a library, buy used, or start with the much cheaper 2025 *The Magic Theorem*, which expands and modernizes the introductory Part I material. But if you can comfortably afford *The Symmetries of Things*, I strongly recommend buying it rather than merely borrowing it. It is exactly the sort of mathematical book that earns permanent shelf space.

—Recommendation written for the Conway project
